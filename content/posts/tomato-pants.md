Two years later, I saw redhat's pants on my feed again. It was July now—coming up on tomato season—perfect timing. I went over to pick up some fabric after work. I grabbed one yard of red and one yard of green cotton fabric. I headed home and thought about the plainness of having just one type of tomato all over my pants. How drab. Around a month and a half after getting the idea to work on this, and two years after seeing the original post, I could get started.

## Pant Prep

To get a better idea of how I can make my pants I looked up redhat's Instagram feed and studied the work that she's done. In one of her posts I saw that the technique she was using is called appliqué. I watched a couple of videos on appliqué on YouTube to see how people on different sewing machines went about it. I learned that redhat used a blanket stitch on her pants to get her distinctive look, but my sewing machine didn't have that. I did have a zigzag stitch, which is another commonly used decorative stitch. That's what I ended up going with for my pants.

A YouTube video recommended washing new fabrics before starting to work on them. Fabric stretches out as it's worn, and according to some Reddit posts fabric from the factory is filthy. It seemed believable enough. So I washed mine. I didn't want to finish the project only to discover that my fabric shrank in the first wash, that could cause my stitches to pucker up, or rip entirely.

I remembered from my 6 hours of sewing instruction that the raw edges of fabric frays. And the people at the fabric store really cut the cuss out of my fabric with the dullest scissors mankind had ever produced, so my fabric was already fraying. I needed to secure the edges to keep them from getting worse in the wash. I didn't have a serger—which puts a clean finish on the edges of the fabric—and I didn't have pinking shears—an old-fashioned way of cleaning up the edges of clothes—so I ran a zigzag stitch over the edges of the fabric to hold it in place in the wash.

{{< figure src="/img/tomato-pants/IMG_0629.jpeg" class="vert" alt="Sewing-machine presser foot beside a loose zigzag stitch along the raw edge of red fabric, with green fabric underneath." >}}

I sucked at it. But I got practice with how the needle moved over the width of the stitch, and how to follow the line of the stitch, which would come in handy later when I needed to zigzag around the tomatoes. I'm pretty sure I didn't do it the right way above.

Alright, fabric washed. I washed mine on cool—the general rule is to wash them on whatever setting you'd wash the final piece. I wash my jeans on cool to prevent as much color fading as I can. It's time to put the pants and fabric up to dry.

{{< figure src="/img/tomato-pants/IMG_0635.jpeg" class="vert" alt="Colored fabric pieces and jeans draped over a folding drying rack." >}}

While they're drying, there's a part that's easy to miss if you've never done appliqué before—You need a way to stabilize the work, interfacing, something to give some _structure_ to your patches. Otherwise you're risking your stitches puckering as the needle pierces your patch. What's even better is to use interfacing that has glue on both sides. The general term for this is a 'fusible web.' But it's basically interfacing with glue on both sides. I used "Heat and Bond" for this project. Redhat has recommended Lite Steam-A-Seam, if you're deciding for yourself I'd suggest trying what she recommended. From seeing her posts, it looks like "Lite Steam-A-Seam" has a more permanent glue than "Heat and Bond". My fusible definitely worked, and it doesn't claim to be permanent, but it can be flimsy, and has a tendency to start peeling if you're really working on your fabric.

Using the fusible web interfacing allowed me to cut patches and glue them onto the pants temporarily while I stitch them down.

I sketched out tomatoes and vines for a couple of hours. Seeing other people's work gave me some confidence—the patch doesn't need to look perfect—the final piece is a patch, not a drawing, as long as you get the idea of a tomato across, it'll look decent. I would suggest working on larger patches than you might initially think. Stitching really small appliqué takes longer, and you'll need more patches on your project. And if you have pointed edges, like on tomato tops, that'll be especially annoying.

The fabric is dried now. The next step is to iron the fabric to get rid of any wrinkles. I'm not going to use the whole yard of fabric, so I eyeball an amount I think is appropriate, and I cut off a piece. I cut off an equal amount of interfacing to match. There's a sticky side of the interfacing that gets laid on top of the fabric, that gets ironed, and then the glue is bonded to the fabric.

I let the glue sit for a second, and then grab some of the templates I've got out. I use a regular pen and trace out designs onto the paper backing. Since this paper backing is going to get peeled off to expose the glue underneath, you don't have to worry about the pen marks. You can see in these pictures that I did a poor job of tracing out the templates. This is fine, it helps make each tomato look unique, even though I used 4 or 5 templates to cut them all out.

{{< exhibit class="two-cl" >}}
{{< figure src="/img/tomato-pants/IMG_0656.jpeg" alt="Tomato shapes traced on paper backing, with orange fabric cutouts and scissors on a cutting mat." >}}
{{< figure src="/img/tomato-pants/IMG_0726.jpeg" alt="Branching tomato stems traced on paper-backed green fabric, with cutout stems and scissors below." >}}

I didn't bother aligning these patterns with the grain of the fabric. My thinking was: These are patches, so I don't _think_ they'll be under the same strain as the underlying denim. I'll post an update after a couple of months of wear to see how this ends up aging. In the pictures of the herringbone-patterned tomato stems you can see they're not aligned with the fabric they're cut out of. I like the look, it gives it some more visual interest, and by ignoring the grain line I'm able to pack on a bunch of templates really close to each other and save on wasted fabric. If you're making garments, you need to make sure the patterns are cut with attention paid to the grain line. It affects things like stretch, and if you have any patterns in your fabric you need to make sure the grain lines match up if you want a clean result. If you notice on fast fashion clothes, sometimes details like the panels of a shirt having designs that don't align... it bothers the cuss out of me. But I digress—back to the pants.

Now it was time to start cutting out all the tomatoes and stems. I batched these together in long sessions because switching between sewing and cutting would be annoying.

### Seam Ripping

If you haven't sewn before, it may not be immediately obvious why you'd do this. If I left the leg was left as-is and put the patch on top, I would end up sewing the patch and both sides of the legs together in a triple layer. A.K.A. I would sew the legs shut. So I seam rip the pants in order to keep it at one layer, so only the patch ends up on top of the single leg. At the very end I sew the panels back together. This step can seem scary, but it's not too bad! It's just one long straight stitch.

{{< exhibit class="two-cl" >}}
{{< figure src="/img/tomato-pants/IMG_0644.jpeg" alt="Inside of a denim trouser leg with the side seam opened near the hem." >}}
{{< figure src="/img/tomato-pants/IMG_0646.jpeg" alt="Close-up of a seam ripper separating stitches along the edge of a denim seam." >}}
{{< /exhibit >}}

If you're new to using a seam ripper, you might not know this, but the red dot is supposed to face *into* the fabric. The pointy bit is used to get the first one or two stitches started, but afterwards you flip around the seam ripper so that the red dot faces into the fabric and the sharp bit stays pointed away. The blunt red dot safely glides against the fabric while the sharp cutting edge slides against the stitches.

To figure out what seams to cut, flip the pants inside out so you can take a look at where the seams bring together the panels. On a typical pair of pants you'll find flat seams, topstitched seams, and flat-felled seams. There are more, but those are the most common on casual wear. Flat-felled seams are a pain, since the fabric is folded in on itself, so I look for plain seams. In the picture below you can see two types of plain seams. You know how people talk about the selvedge? Like selvedge denim? Sometimes you can see it as the little ribbon on the cuff of jeans? That's showing a flat seam. The selvedge on jeans refers to the fact that it's using the very edge of a fabric that's off a loom, and fabric from the loom has that finished edge. You won't see the edge of the fabric if it's a cut off a much larger loom and not from the edge. If I remember correctly newer textile mills product fabric that is larger in size than older manufacturing methods, so there's less 'selvedge' to go around, so you just don't see it as much. You can have selvedge jeans that are garbage, just like you can find non-selvedge jeans that are constructed well—I'm getting off the rails again. Back to the pants.

The jeans I used for this aren't selvedge, so the flat seam looks more typical to what you'll find on most garments.

There's an art to stitching zigzags on corners that I still haven't nailed. When working with partially seam ripped pants like this, there's a lot of tedium involved with positioning the fabric and feeding it through the sewing machine. At the beginning I had a poor handle on how I should be handling the jeans and it strained my shoulder as I fumbled around with the denim. Towards the end of the project I learned to pay more attention to where the weight of the pants was, and how they were feeding through the machine, rather than fighting against the fabric that wanted to pull the pants away from the machine. You want to make sure the feed dogs aren't fighting against the weight of the project.

Yes, if you were to just take the panels apart, this would've been much easier. But... I didn't want to do that. There's bartacking I would've had to reapply and I was lazy lazy lazy. If you're not lazy, you can do this. Or honestly just seam rip past the bartack, you don't need to completely take the panel off.

Now that the tomatoes were beginning to go on, most of the work is straightforward: Take some patches, place them in a place I liked, peel off the backing paper, iron them on, then sew them in place.

The tomato stems were a pain. And the placement of this vine was going to make it even more painful. The vine was near the bartack (near the pocket), which was as far as I seam ripped to. I wouldn't have perfectly flat panels to sew on top of. I had to be careful and adjust the fabric to make sure things like the pocket bags wouldn't get sewn together. Denim is not very flexible, so maneuvering the fabric through the throat of the machine (that's the hole between the arm and the sewing surface of the machine) can be tiring if you're doing it repeatedly. The vines did come out great, and are my favorite part of the pant, but every part of them took so much longer than the other tomatoes. 

The first vine took me two hours to get through.

Because the fusible interfacing isn't super sticky and the panels didn't lie that flat, as soon as I ironed them on, I put a stack of coffee table books on top and let it sit overnight. It helped a bit, but I skipped it for the second set of vines and tolerated a bit of peeling.

{{< figure src="/img/tomato-pants/IMG_0696.jpeg" class="vert" alt="A branching green vine with red and orange tomatoes outlined in pale zigzag stitching on denim." >}}

I finished a couple tomatoes and was feeling satisfied that my appliqué was making progress, so I experimented with different aspects of the construction. I was going through a lot of thread, and knew that I'd have to order more before I could finish the project. I saw online that there were different sizes of Gütermann thread, and I had some generic "sew-all" thread from them. Turns out "sew-all" retail thread is the same  thickness and material as Mara 100 polyester, and there are many sizes of Mara thread from Gütermann. I was satisfied with the results so far, but I thought a _bit_ more thickness could add more of a 'look'. Buying Mara 100 online ends up being much cheaper than the sew-all thread you'll find in most stores. I recommend picking up the 1,000m thread, you can use it for most projects. They've got every color under the sun for that line, and they sell a swatch kit.

I read online people suggesting to only run a thicker thread on top and keep the smaller thread for the bottom. I don't remember the reasoning behind it, but I thought "why not" and just tried it out. Mara 100—the thinner thread—for the bottom thread, and Mara 70—one size up—for the top thread.

It's nice. I like the thicker thread look. You may have to adjust your tension for the thicker thread, but the jump from Mara 100 to Mara 70 isn't super significant. I made it _slightly_ less tense, but didn't notice any different and went back to my original tension—3.

When passing the finished areas over the light, I noticed the holes that the needle was leaving behind were pretty big. This whole time I used the generic 'jeans' needle that came with my sewing machine. Turns out that needle was size 100/16, which is definitely meant to be used with _real_ denim, not this cheap lightweight denim I was using, and likely for regular stitching like stitching denim panels together, not thin patches of cotton on top of denim. I ordered some 90/14 and 80/12 needles. Honestly, I can't really see the difference.

### How long the project took

I'm spoilering the next section to prevent influencing anyone from taking on the project. If you want to take on this project, skip over to the next section!

I kept a journal of how much time I spent on the project, I'll break it down:
* Sourcing Fabric (2 hours)
* Prepping Fabric (4 hours—some of this was my fault, maybe should've been 2)
* Stenciling, Sketching (~2 hours)
* Cutting out the tomatoes & stems (3 hours, I cut too many)
* Ironing (~2 hours)
* Seam Ripping pants (1 hour (gotta clean up the thread too))
* Sewing them back up, pressing (~45 minutes)
* Sewing tomatoes ~18 hours

Total Time: ~33 hours.

So, when I thought Redhat's pants were 'expensive', if I just take the NYC hourly minimum wage of \$17.00 (2026 minimum) that would bring my total cost to \$561! And that's not including the cost of the pants, the fact I had all this sewing equipment, the machine, fabric, notions and RENT.

Typical markups from production cost to wholesale is ~100%, which would put my total market cost at almost $1,200 dollars. And that would only be to a wholesaler. There's another markup of another 100% to bring it to retail.

I started 'officially' on the 16th of September and didn't get done until October 8th, and I spent on average 2 hours per day doing _something_ for this project. I lost passion for it around 3/4 of the way in, when it was just tedium; sewing a tomato, ironing, sewing another tomato, ironing a patch in place, and sewing some more.

I highly recommend it :) If you want to take on a project like this, here's what you're gonna need:

## Bill of Materials

* Sewing Machine
* Fabric
* Fabric Shears
* Iron w/ board (you can use a wool mat if you've got it)
* Jeans Needle (Universal Needle might work for looser jean weaves?)
* Thread, polyester or cotton.
  * Choose thicker thread for more visual impact. Stick with polyester by default. It's stronger, and won't take on dye in case your patches bleed dye.
* Double Sided Fusible
* Pins or Clips to hold Fabric in place
* Pens + Paper to make templates (If you're good at visualizing shapes, you can just freehand cut out your shapes!)

### Sewing Machine Settings

> Zigzag Stitch 
\
> Length --- 1
\
> Tension --- 3
\
> Width --- between 3 and 4

You *can* sew appliqué by hand, but if you're going for something with a lot of patches, it's going to take an eternity to finish. I used a zigzag stitch for my pants, but any decorative stitch or even straight stitches can be used here.

Don't let the seam ripping scare you. I purposely chose a pair of pants that have very simple construction because it's straightforward to repair. On jeans they tend to have a plain seam (like what I showed earlier in the blog) on the outside of the pant. If you're leaning towards workwear, you might find more robust stitching. You can always rip these and put them back together. It's just more work.

If you're worried about seam ripping, you can start with some garments that can be laid flat, like a thin jacket. I would suggest picking a garment that has no stretch, since I think those fabrics are a pain to work with, but it could be possible.

Play around with the stitch width. While having a smaller width leads to cleaner lines, you really have to make sure all the stitches land in the right place and work a bit slower. If I were more skilled, I would have stayed with the smaller width the entire time. But when I got tired and just wanted to be done with a patch, I opened the width up to 4 and just sent it.
